Station UE 16

Sound, haptics, language

Three layers that sit on top of a working game. Each is a place where Unreal already provides what the Unity project had to build: voice management, haptic effects, and text that changes language without being rebuilt.

At the end
29 cues played in the room where they happen, a buzz in the controller that caused them, and every line of copy in English, Japanese, Spanish and French.
On the headset
A piece clicks into place under your hand. Change the language on the wrist menu and every sign changes at once.
Unity equivalent
Milestone XR10. Committed, but not yet verified on a headset in the Unity project.

Part one: sound

The cue vocabulary

Game code never names a sound file. It plays a cue, named for what happened: Grab, Place, Error, LineCleared, Stamp. A library asset maps each cue to a sound, a volume and a haptic. There are 29, numbered 1 to 29.

Append onlyThe library stores each slot by the cue's number. Insert or reorder an enum value and every later slot gets the wrong sound. A test pins the numbers. Port the enum with its explicit values and port the test.

Every cue is placed in the room: a piece cue plays from the piece, a board cue from the board, a UI cue from the panel pressed. XRCueMap.For(DropResult) decides which cue a grab or release plays, and it is engine-free and fully tested.

Unreal concepts: the audio engine does the pooling

The Unity player keeps a pool of eight audio sources so cues can overlap, and one more that loops. Unreal manages voices itself. You describe the rules in assets:

Unity codeUnreal asset or call
Eight pooled sources, oldest reusedA Sound Concurrency asset: max count 8, resolution "Stop Oldest"
Logarithmic rolloff, full volume within 0.3 m, silent by 6 mA Sound Attenuation asset: inner radius 30, falloff distance 570, natural sound falloff
Doppler offNothing to do. There is none unless you add it.
Multiply every voice by the effects volumeA Sound Class for effects, with a Sound Mix override setting its volume
A looping source that follows the train and fadesUGameplayStatics::SpawnSoundAttached, which returns a UAudioComponent with FadeIn, FadeOut and SetPaused
PlayOneShot at a positionUGameplayStatics::PlaySoundAtLocation

A tabletop game is heard from half a metre away, which is why the attenuation is so tight.

Steps

  1. Port the cue enum, the map and their tests to TsugiCore: ETsugiCue, FTsugiCueMap::For, Fanfare, IsFelt, and XRCueTests.

  2. Declare the library.

    Import the clips from the Unity project's Assets/05.Audio/. Which clip fills which slot is in Assets/03.Data/XR/Audio/XRCues.asset; open it as text. Assign each imported sound to the effects Sound Class.

  3. Write the player. A world subsystem is a good home: it exists whenever a level does, and anything with a world can reach it.

  4. Call it where Unity does. Search the Unity XR code for XRCuePlayer.Play; there are a few dozen call sites and each names a cue and a position. The main ones: the piece hands on every grab and release, the press component on every button, roundels, the board on a line cleared and on the final piece, the train's start and loop, the arrival's stars, stamp and fanfare, the wrist menu opening, the board being placed, and the tutorial's chime.

  5. Connect the volume setting. Create a Sound Mix. When the effects volume preference changes, and once at start, call UGameplayStatics::SetSoundMixClassOverride for the effects class with volume Level / 10, and push the mix with PushSoundMixModifier. The music setting is stored and drives nothing, because there is no music. Leave it that way and say so in a comment.

Part two: haptics

Unity sends an amplitude and a duration per cue. Unreal's unit is a haptic feedback effect asset: a curve of amplitude and frequency over time. Author three or four in the editor, such as a short tick, a firmer tap and a double pulse for errors, and reference them from the cue library with a scale. That is less data than Unity's table and easier to tune by feel.

Only fourteen cues are felt, per XRCueMap.IsFelt: the piece actions, the UI presses, the map selects and placing the board. Board, train and fanfare cues are heard only.

Design ruleTracked hands have nothing to buzz. Haptics are decoration, and nothing may be understandable only through them. Every felt cue also has a sound and something to see.

Not confirmedWhether PlayHapticEffect reaches Touch controllers through Epic's OpenXR path on 5.8 without extra setup was not tested. If nothing buzzes, check that the mapping context includes the haptic output binding for each hand, and look at Meta's haptics module as an alternative.

Part three: language

Unreal concepts

FText is the only string type that localises. An FText is not the words; it is a reference to an entry that resolves to words in the current culture. Change the culture and every FText on screen changes, with no rebuild.

That removes a whole mechanism from the port. The Unity XR code raises XRText.Changed and every panel redraws itself in the new language. In Unreal, text blocks bound to localised text update themselves. Only text you have formatted into a final string needs refreshing.

Unity LocalizationUnreal
A String Table, keyedA String Table asset, keyed. FText::FromStringTable(TableId, Key)
GetLocalizedString(table, key, args)FText::Format(Pattern, Args…). {0} placeholders work as they are.
Locales en, ja, es, frCultures, in the Localization Dashboard
LocalizationSettings.SelectedLocale = …FInternationalization::Get().SetCurrentCulture(TEXT("ja"))
A baked SDF atlas for Japanese, rebuilt whenever copy changesA composite font with a Japanese sub-family. Glyphs are rasterised on demand. Nothing to bake.
Proper nouns kept out of the tableFText::AsCultureInvariant(Name)

The font atlas is worth a moment. The Unity project's notes on its Japanese atlas run to several paragraphs of hard-won detail: a deprecated static mode, a flag that discards the bake at build time, metrics that made every label 1.875 times too tall. None of that exists here. It is the clearest case in the course of the engine's own design removing a problem.

Steps

  1. Get the copy out of Unity. All of it, in four languages, is in one file: Assets/01.Scripts/XR/Editor/XRCopy.cs. Its table is internal, so the exporter must sit in the same folder.

  2. Create the string table. From xr_copy.csv, make a two-column file with the header Key,SourceString holding the key and the English. In the Content Browser, create a String Table asset, ST_Tsugi, and import that CSV into it. English is the source language.

  3. Use it. In the widget designer, bind each static label to its string table entry. In C++, look text up by key:

    The coach's Key from lesson 15 goes straight into TsugiText::Get.

  4. Set up the Localization Dashboard. Tools > Localization Dashboard. On the Game target: gather from string table assets under your localisation folder; set English as native; add ja, es and fr. Press Gather Text. Each culture now lists every key as untranslated.

  5. Bring the translations in. Export each culture as a .po file from the dashboard, fill in each entry's translation from the matching column of xr_copy.csv with a short script, import the files back, and press Compile Text. Write the script so it fails loudly on a key it cannot find in either direction: that is your "every key exists in every locale" check, which Unity has as StringTableTests.

  6. Build the font. Import Barlow-Regular.ttf, Barlow-Medium.ttf, BarlowCondensed-SemiBold.ttf and NotoSansJP-Medium.otf from the Unity project's Assets/02.Graphics/Fonts/. Their licence files are beside them; ship those too. Create one Font asset, F_Tsugi, as a composite font: Barlow as the default family, and a sub-font family using Noto for the Japanese ranges: hiragana, katakana, CJK ideographs, CJK punctuation and full-width forms.

    Leave the sub-family's culture list empty so the ranges apply in every language. The settings page lists each language in its own words, so 日本語 must draw while the game is in French.

    Carry this overUse the static Medium instance of Noto, as Unity does. A variable Noto file exposes only its thinnest weight to many font loaders, and the whole Japanese UI comes out hairline.

  7. Switch language at runtime.

    Text blocks update by themselves. For anything you format in C++, such as the clock or "3 / 9 CLEARED", re-run the code that set it. Listen for FTextLocalizationManager::Get().OnTextRevisionChangedEvent and have the shell redraw the current view, as XRGame.OnLocaleChanged does.

  8. Package the languages. In Project Settings > Packaging: add en, ja, es and fr to Localizations to Package, and set Internationalization Support to an option that includes CJK. Then test on the device, not in the editor.

Acceptance checks

  • Tsugi.Core.Cue tests pass, including the one that pins every cue's number.
  • Placing a piece makes a sound that comes from the piece: with eyes closed I can point at it.
  • An illegal drop and a steered drop sound different.
  • Ten quick drops in a row never cut the audio out or stack into noise.
  • With controllers, a grab buzzes the hand that grabbed and not the other. With bare hands nothing errors.
  • Effects volume 0 on the wrist menu silences every cue and the train loop; 10 restores them.
  • The five cues with no clip (Puff, Burst, Whistle, TrainLoop, TrainHurry) are silent and log no errors.
  • Pausing during the train run pauses its loop; resuming continues it.
  • Changing language on the wrist menu changes the signboard, the wrist menu, the callout and the placement sign immediately, on the device.
  • In Japanese, no label shows a missing-glyph box, and no label is oddly tall or thin.
  • In every language the settings row shows English, 日本語, Español and Français correctly.
  • Station and line names are identical in all four languages.
  • The language chosen survives a relaunch.

Pitfalls

  • Translated in the editor, English on the headset. The cultures were not added to the packaging settings, or the compiled text was not rebuilt after an import. This is the most common localisation bug in Unreal.
  • An FString in the middle. Convert localised text to FString and back and it is frozen in the language it had at that moment. Keep FText all the way to the widget.
  • A cue per frame. Hover must not play a sound each frame the hand is over a button. Play on the change, not on the state.
  • Sound from the wrong place. A UI cue plays from the fingertip that pressed, not from the centre of the sign. The press component hands you the hand; use its fingertip position.
  • The store recommends, and does not require, spatialised 3D audio (VRC.Quest.Audio.1). Positioned sounds with attenuation meet the spirit of it. Meta's audio plugin adds head-related spatialisation if your team wants it later.

Knowledge check

Where did the Unity project's eight-voice pool go in the Unreal port?

  • It is ported as a pool of eight audio components.
  • It became a Sound Concurrency asset; the audio engine manages the voices.
  • It is dropped, so only one cue plays at a time.

The pool existed in Unity because a one-shot voice has no handle. Unreal's concurrency settings express the same rule declaratively.

The player changes language. Which text on the sign needs code to refresh it?

  • All of it.
  • None of it.
  • Only text built by formatting, such as "3 / 9 CLEARED".

A plain localised FText re-resolves itself. A formatted result was computed once from the pattern in the old language.

A new cue, TrayMove, is needed. Where does it go in the enum?

  • With the other tray and piece cues, near the top.
  • At the end, with the next unused number.
  • Anywhere; the library looks cues up by name.

The library stores slots by number. Inserting shifts every later slot onto the wrong sound.
