Port Tsugi XR from Unity to Unreal

Nineteen lessons. Each one teaches a piece of how Unreal Engine works, then has you rebuild one slice of the Quest edition of Tsugi from the Unity source. At the last station you hold a signed build for Meta Standalone.

You are given
The Unity project on branch feat/MetaXR: the engine-free rules, the XR edition, 216 levels, and about 390 unit tests.
You build
The same game in Unreal Engine 5.8: C++ for rules, saving and geometry, Blueprint for the pawn, tuning and wiring.
You ship
An APK for Quest 3 and Quest 3S that passes the store's Virtual Reality Checks.

The game, in one paragraph

Tsugi is a track-laying puzzle. A board is a grid, 6×6 to 8×8. Every row and column carries a number: how many cells in that line must hold track. A train enters at S and must leave at E. A piece connects two sides of its cell, and a piece laid beside existing track must join it. You win when one continuous track runs from S to E and every number is met exactly.

The Quest edition puts that board on your real table in passthrough, six centimetres to a cell. You pinch pieces out of a tray and drop them on the platform. A green ghost says the drop is legal, a red one says it will fly back. Menus are pressed with a fingertip. There are no rays anywhere.

How each lesson works

  1. Goal. What exists at the end, and what you can see on the headset.
  2. Unreal concepts. Explained for someone who already knows Unity.
  3. Read first. The Unity files for this slice. Read them before you write anything.
  4. Steps. Class skeletons, and the hard parts in full, with the Unity original beside the Unreal version.
  5. Your port. The exercise. You write the rest from the Unity source.
  6. Acceptance checks. Tick them only when you have seen them pass. The repo's own rule applies here too: nothing is done until a human has verified it.
  7. Pitfalls and a knowledge check. Several pitfalls were learned the hard way on the Unity headset checks and carry straight over.

    What you need before lesson 1

    • A Windows PC that can run Unreal Engine 5.8 and about 150 GB of free disk.
    • A Meta Quest 3 or 3S in developer mode, a USB-C cable, and a Meta developer account.
    • The Unity repository checked out on feat/MetaXR. You do not need Unity installed to read it, but lesson 4 exports the levels from the Unity Editor, so one person on the team does.
    • Working C# and a rough idea of C++. Lesson 2 covers what Unreal adds on top of the language.

    The state of the Unity original

    The Unity edition is not finished, and you should not port a gap as if it were a feature.

    • XR9 (tutorial) and XR10 (sound, haptics, language) are committed but not yet verified on a headset. Their boxes in Docs/XR-PRD.md are unticked.
    • Music arrived after these lessons were written (2026-10-06): two background loops and a jingle per line, in XRMusicPlan.cs and XRMusic.cs. Where a lesson says the music volume drives nothing, port those two files instead.
    • Five cues have no clip: Puff, Burst, Whistle, TrainLoop, TrainHurry.
    • XR11 (release readiness) has not started. No code sets the refresh rate or foveation. Lessons 17 and 18 are therefore new work, with no Unity answer to copy.
    • The PRD has drifted from the code in places. Where they disagree, the code is the reference. Each lesson names code, never the PRD alone.
    • quickTestRails is 3 in the Unity scene. It pre-lays all but three rails of every board, as a testing aid. Do not carry that default into your build.
